Study scope and data products

Paired spectral acquisition

An ordinary RGB camera records red, green and blue. A hyperspectral camera records a narrow reflectance measurement at hundreds of wavelengths for every image location. FX10 covers visible and near-infrared light; FX17 extends into short-wave infrared. Together they describe pigment, leaf structure and water-related spectral behaviour that normal colour photography cannot show.

This dataset contains three separate potted plants in one scan. The analysis compares their spatial patterns, but it does not contain labels such as healthy, diseased, watered or dry.

How the raw scan became these results

The sequence matters: an index is only meaningful after the sensor values are calibrated, plant pixels are isolated, and the two cameras are brought onto comparable spectral and spatial coordinates.

1 · Raw scansFX10 and FX17 record sensor intensity.
2 · CalibrateWhite and dark panels convert intensity to reflectance.
3 · MaskNDVI and NWACI separate plants from the scene.
4 · Cross-calibrateShared wavelengths correct FX17 scale and offset.
5 · RegisterSIFT and RANSAC align the two camera views.
6 · MergeAligned spectra form one 398–1720 nm cube.
7 · AnalyseFourteen indices map relative plant properties.

Core hyperspectral results

Figure 1

Raw intensity versus calibrated reflectance

Raw panels show sensor brightness, which depends on lighting and camera response. Calibrated panels estimate the fraction of incoming light reflected at each wavelength by subtracting the dark reference and scaling to the white reference.

FX10 can be displayed in roughly natural colour because it includes visible wavelengths. FX17 is shown in false colour because 1050–1600 nm is invisible to human eyes; the colours reveal contrast, not the plant's real appearance.

Figure 2

Plant isolation and background removal

The software calculated NDVI for FX10 and NWACI for FX17, automatically selected thresholds, then cleaned small holes and isolated noise. White mask pixels mean “use this plant pixel”; black means “exclude this background pixel”.

FX10 retained 15.4% of its scene at threshold 0.300. FX17 retained 18.2% at threshold 0.241. Different percentages are expected because camera framing and wavelengths differ.

Figure 3

The mean spectral fingerprint

The horizontal axis is wavelength in nanometres. The vertical axis is mean reflectance of the retained plant pixels. The visible pigment region, the sharp red-edge rise near 700 nm, the NIR plateau and strong SWIR water-absorption features are all plant-like spectral structure.

FX10 and FX17 overlap around 936–1004 nm. A linear gain of 1.277 and offset of -0.188 reduced overlap RMSE from 0.0055 to 0.0019. This means their average curves agree better; it does not prove every individual pixel is perfectly calibrated.

Figure 4

Spatial registration: making the cameras look at the same place

SIFT keypoints are distinctive visual landmarks. Matches pair similar landmarks between cameras. RANSAC inliers are the pairs that agree on one geometric transformation after outliers are rejected.

The workflow found 408 FX10 and 461 FX17 keypoints, 194 good matches and 190 inliers. The average reprojection error was 0.63 pixels. Mutual information rose from 1.290 to 1.294; the small increase is supporting evidence, while the match count, error and overlay provide the clearer alignment evidence.

The yellow overlay should closely follow the plant shapes. Registration is essential before attaching spectra from the two sensors to the same leaf location.

Figure 5

Regional NDVI across three vertical scan regions

The image was divided into top, middle and bottom thirds for regional statistics. In this scene those thirds mostly correspond to three different plants; therefore, “upper”, “middle” and “lower” describe image position rather than canopy height within one plant.

Mean NDVI was 0.665, 0.590 and 0.648. The middle scan region was lower, but the absence of treatment or health labels prevents causal interpretation.

Supported findings and scientific limitations

Evidence supported by this stage

  • Both cameras produced usable, plant-like spectra across their stated ranges.
  • The background masks isolate the three plant regions cleanly enough for exploratory analysis.
  • FX10 and FX17 average spectra agree substantially better after overlap correction.
  • The two camera views were aligned with 190 consistent feature matches and about 0.63-pixel average error.
  • A registered 427-band plant cube spanning approximately 398–1720 nm was produced.
  • The fourteen maps reveal spatial variation that can guide later sampling and modelling.

Scientific limitations

  • The indices cannot diagnose disease, drought, nutrient deficiency or plant health without labelled ground truth.
  • Index values are not percentages or laboratory concentrations.
  • The three scan regions cannot be ranked biologically without labels and controlled treatments.
  • The hyperspectral-only stage did not contain 3D geometry; the later RGB-D fusion sections add measured depth and global placement.
  • Cross-camera and cross-modal registration are validated approximations, not proof of perfect correspondence everywhere.
  • Biological conclusions require reference targets, repeat scans and laboratory or treatment measurements.

Validation interpretation

MAPE means the average absolute error expressed as a percentage of the manual measurement. Lower is better. The three reported aggregate values show close agreement for plant height and the specifically matched leaf dimensions in this validation set.

The underlying report retains every plant and leaf comparison, so the aggregate value does not obscure item-level differences.

Validation limitations

The leaf result is a guided comparison: a human identified corresponding leaves and landmarks. Automatic cross-view leaf identity is still future work. The canopy-area and segment outputs are useful geometric descriptions, but they are not biological ground truth.

Cross-modal fusion status

Feasibility finding and completed prototype · recorded 31 August 2026

Feasibility demonstrated with the captured data

The matching RGB-D run contains 945 RGB frames and 945 depth frames, calibrated colour and depth intrinsics, an existing ICP reconstruction from 95 sampled frames, a 44,430,791-point merged cloud, and cleaned point clouds for all three plants. The hyperspectral and RGB-D images visibly contain the same three plants, board grid and black-and-white fiducial markers.

The RGB-D folder identifies the run as 12:08:00. Both hyperspectral headers record 02:13:42 UTC, equivalent to approximately 12:13:42 Sydney time: about five minutes and forty-two seconds later. Because the subjects are stationary plants and the scene layout matches, sequential rather than simultaneous capture is acceptable for a prototype, although small leaf movement remains a registration error source.

Inputs already available

  • Calibrated FX10 and FX17 data and a merged 427-band cube.
  • RGB images, aligned depth images and camera intrinsics.
  • ICP-based scene reconstruction and individual plant point clouds.
  • Strong cross-modal anchors: the board, grid and fiducial markers.
  • Reference frames: Coleus 330, fuzzy kalanchoe 540 and succulent/jade 750.

Implemented in this prototype

  • FX10-to-RGB-D registration for all three selected frames.
  • Depth backprojection from matched pixels into metric XYZ.
  • Full 427-band attachment plus five index-coloured 3D exports.
  • Depth coverage, reprojection and specimen-surface validation.
  • Direct recovery of the three transforms needed for global ICP placement.

Role and scope of ICP

ICP aligns successive RGB-D point clouds to one another. It creates the 3D geometry but cannot directly align a two-dimensional hyperspectral cube to that geometry. Cross-modal fusion therefore needs a separate image-registration step. After an FX10 pixel is matched to an RGB-D pixel, its depth gives camera-space XYZ; the saved ICP pose then places that spectral point in the common reconstruction coordinate system.

Implemented fusion workflow

  1. Coleus proof of concept: registered the upper hyperspectral component to RGB-D frame 330 and exported a depth-derived NDVI surface.
  2. Complete spectral attachment: associated the full 427-band spectrum and five indices with each valid 3D point.
  3. Other plants: repeated the validated workflow for fuzzy kalanchoe frame 540 and succulent/jade frame 750.
  4. Global scene placement: recovered the three required transforms directly against the legacy scene and exported global models.
  5. Research outputs: generated registration overlays, numerical validation, downloadable PLYs and an interactive viewer.

Coverage limitation: the hyperspectral cameras primarily measured the upper visible surfaces. Occluded sides and leaf undersides remain grey/unmeasured. Any value propagated to those points would be an inference, not a measurement.

What was corrected during implementation

The first extension used equal top/middle/bottom row thirds. Visual validation showed that those ranges overlap the plant silhouettes, mixing Coleus pixels into the fuzzy plant and fuzzy pixels into the jade plant. The production run now selects the actual connected plant components from the NDVI mask. Degenerate local homographies are rejected by inlier, reprojection-error and mapped-area checks; mapped pixels are deduplicated, separated from the background depth layer and finally required to lie within 20 mm of the corresponding cleaned 3D plant cloud.

Fusion coverage

The prototype fusion is complete for all retained measured points: 10,485 XYZ points each carry the full 427-band calibrated spectrum, five displayed indices, their source hyperspectral pixel and their corresponding RGB-D pixel. Those points are also placed in the common ICP scene.

Coverage limitation

This is not complete surface coverage of every leaf. The hyperspectral cameras mainly observed upper-visible surfaces, so sides, undersides and occluded areas remain grey and have no invented spectrum. Registration is measured and validated, but remains an approximation affected by parallax and the five-minute capture gap.

Display and precision note: all measured spectral points are selectable. Grey scene/specimen geometry is deterministically sampled for responsiveness. Spectra in the local viewer are stored at 16-bit display precision; the downloadable NPZ files retain the original float32 arrays.

Global placement inside the legacy ICP scene

Missing-pose workaround completed · 31 August 2026

Direct recovery of missing global poses

The legacy run omitted its cumulative pose files, but frames 330, 540 and 750 were included in the every-tenth-frame ICP sequence. Each cleaned plant cloud therefore has a matching surface already embedded in the merged scene. A rigid camera-to-global transform was recovered by registering each cleaned plant cloud directly to the saved 300,000-point scene sample, then applying that transform to every fused spectral model.

This provides the placement required for the three plants. It does not recreate the missing complete trajectory of all 95 cameras.

Engineering fix for future runs

Modify the reconstructor so it writes the cumulative 4×4 matrix after every accepted frame, together with frame index, fitness and RMSE. Then global placement is a direct matrix application, and this recovery step is unnecessary.
